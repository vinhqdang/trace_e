\ABSTRACT{%
\textit{Research Questions.} Blocking the further spread of an
already-seeded rumor or epidemic on a network, by disabling a small number
of accounts or individuals under a fixed budget, is normally solved as a
one-shot decision: the entire budget is spent before anything about how
the outbreak actually unfolds is observed. We ask two questions this
one-shot view leaves open. First, can a decision maker who instead commits
the budget gradually, as the outbreak reveals itself, ever do worse than
committing everything up front, and by how much better can they do?
Second, since every existing method for choosing which accounts to disable
is judged by randomly simulating the outbreak many times rather than by
computing its true effect, how far off can that simulation-based judgment
be from the truth?

\textit{Methodology and Results.} We answer the first question with DEFER,
a general procedure that upgrades any existing one-shot blocking method
into a gradual one, and we prove three things about it: the pure-deferral
variant never leaves the decision maker worse off than committing
everything immediately; a simple additional rule for deciding when to
commit an exposed target versus wait is the best possible such rule under
local conditions, though (a closed-form counterexample shows) it does not
inherit the same never-worse guarantee once several deferred targets
compete for the same budget; and gradual commitment's advantage over
committing all at once can be made arbitrarily large, growing with the
maximum number of people any single infected individual can pass the
outbreak to. We answer the second question with TWIG, which computes the
exact effect of any proposed blocking choice instead of estimating it by
simulation, on networks small enough for exact computation, and use it to
measure -- without simulation noise clouding the measurement -- how often
and how badly greedy blocking misses the truly best choice (exactly
optimal in 56 of 60 test instances, mean excess $2.2\%$, worst case
$84.6\%$).

\textit{Importance and Implications.} Public-health authorities and
platform moderators rarely have to decide everything about an intervention
before it begins; DEFER shows, with a formal guarantee rather than a case
study, that using this flexibility in its pure form is never harmful and
can be highly valuable, and it does so by wrapping methods practitioners
already use rather than by replacing them. TWIG supplies a more systematic
check than prior partial validation on the simulation-based evaluations
the outbreak-blocking literature relies on, at the modest network sizes
exact computation currently admits.
}%

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Misinformation and epidemic-style contagions on networks share a common
downstream control problem once they have already started: given a fixed
set of active or seeded sources and a limited budget of vertices that can be
removed (accounts suspended, individuals quarantined, links cut), which
vertices should be chosen to minimize the eventual spread? This is the
\emph{influence minimization} (IMIN) problem \citep{xie2023,xie2024}, the
natural complement of the much more heavily studied influence
\emph{maximization} problem \citep{kempe2003}: instead of choosing seeds to
spread a cascade, IMIN chooses a small blocking set to contain one. Unlike
maximization, the objective is not supermodular in general, so greedy
selection carries no approximation guarantee, and the problem is NP-hard and
hard to approximate \citep{xie2023}.

Every published IMIN algorithm -- AdvancedGreedy and GreedyReplace
\citep{xie2023,xie2024}, and the Sandwich lower/upper-bound family
\citep{wang2024sandwich} -- is \emph{one-shot}: the entire budget is spent
before the cascade is observed even once. In practice a moderator or
public-health authority observes who has already been exposed, round by
round, and can adapt. This raises two questions not, to our knowledge,
formally answered for IMIN: (i) can adapting to the unfolding cascade only
help, never hurt, relative to any one-shot plan; and (ii) how large can
adaptivity's benefit be, in the worst case? A third, orthogonal problem is
verification: every existing IMIN algorithm is evaluated by Monte-Carlo
simulation, so a reported ``optimality gap'' of greedy selection is
always confounded with sampling noise, with no exact ground truth
available beyond trivial instances.

\subsection*{Contributions}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{DEFER} (Section~\ref{sec:defer}), an adaptive wrapper around
  any one-shot IMIN planner that re-plans each round, protects the
  previous round's uncommitted plan against replacement by a worse one,
  and (optionally) commits a planned vertex early only if a push-down
  inequality shows waiting is not preferable. We prove: deferred
  commitment alone is free (Lemma~\ref{lem:defer-free}); the pure-deferral
  variant, \texttt{DEFER-nopush}, weakly dominates the planner it wraps
  (Theorem~\ref{thm:defer-dominates}); push-down is the myopic optimum for
  the local, single-vertex commit-now/wait decision
  (Lemma~\ref{lem:pushdown}) but, by a closed-form counterexample, does
  not inherit this guarantee once several deferred targets compete for
  the same budget (Remark~\ref{rem:pushdown-counterexample}); and the
  adaptivity gap of IMIN is unbounded, in two distinct senses
  (Theorem~\ref{thm:adaptivity-gap}), with a matching, checkable
  construction.
\item \textbf{TWIG} (Section~\ref{sec:twig}), an exact dynamic program for
  the expected spread of a candidate blocking set, correct on any
  elimination order (Theorem~\ref{thm:twig-correct}) and exponential, for
  a given order, in the width $w$ it realizes -- a pathwidth-type
  parameter, not the graph's treewidth (Section~\ref{sec:treewidth-prelim}) --
  then reformulated via an exact collapse (Theorem~\ref{thm:twig-collapse})
  into a fixed-parameter tractable algorithm in $w$
  (Propositions~\ref{prop:xp-space}--\ref{prop:fpt-space}). We further show
  processing \emph{direction} is a first-class choice
  (Proposition~\ref{prop:direction}), and that no order keeps $w$ bounded
  on trees, since TWIG's linear schedule has no join operation -- so TWIG
  is bounded-pathwidth, not bounded-treewidth
  (Section~\ref{sec:twig-complexity}).
\item An exact, sampling-noise-free measurement of the optimality gap of
  greedy IMIN selection (Section~\ref{sec:twig-empirical}): on graphs small
  enough for TWIG to enumerate, greedy is exactly optimal in 56 of 60
  instances and departs from the true optimum by up to 84.6\% in the worst
  case found -- the first such measurement in the IMIN literature free of
  Monte-Carlo confounds.
\item A benchmark of DEFER against AdvancedGreedy, GreedyReplace, and
  simple heuristics on SNAP-scale contact and collaboration networks
  (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}), together with an auxiliary,
  apples-to-apples comparison on the companion source-detection problem
  used throughout our broader project.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Influence minimization.} \citet{xie2023} introduce IMIN, prove
NP-hardness and inapproximability, and give AdvancedGreedy. \citet{xie2024}
extend this with GreedyReplace and give the network-reliability-flavored
complexity analysis Theorem \ref{thm:twig-correct} builds on; their own
Exact-vs-GreedyReplace check (their Table 3) uses a borrowed exact method
on 100-node subgraphs at up to four budget levels, smaller and less
systematic than the fully proof-verified, 60-instance study of
Section~\ref{sec:twig-empirical}. The Sandwich lower/upper-bound family we
use as the LSBM baseline is instead due to \citet{wang2024sandwich}.

\textbf{Rumor and misinformation blocking before IMIN.} Limiting an
already-spreading process's damage by removing or countering a small
vertex set predates IMIN's formal NP-hardness treatment by over a decade:
\citet{budak2011} counter-seed a rival campaign under independent
cascade; \citet{he2012} extend this to competitive linear threshold;
\citet{nguyen2012} and \citet{wang2013blocking} study node/edge removal
directly, essentially one-shot IMIN without \citet{xie2023}'s later
hardness results; \citet{fan2013} add a removal-cost budget;
\citet{wang2016drimux} and \citet{tong2018} generalize to dynamic and
multi-campaign settings (\citet{tong2017}, below, is a maximization
algorithm and not part of this line); \citet{farajtabar2017} model the
cascade as a Hawkes process steered by reinforcement learning;
\citet{yan2020} study blocker placement under a fixed budget; and
\citet{zareie2021} survey this pre-2021 line. \citet{xiang2024} is a
recent pertinence-constrained variant contemporaneous with
\citet{xie2024}, the direct ancestor of the AG/GR planners DEFER wraps.
None of this line treats commitment timing as a decision variable, the
gap DEFER's adaptive formulation addresses.

\textbf{Exact spread computation.} Computing expected spread exactly is a
network-reliability problem and is \#P-hard in general
\citep{valiant1979,provan1983}. \citet{maehara2017} give an early exact
algorithm via binary decision diagrams. \citet{nakamura2026} give a
linear-time exact algorithm for spread on bounded-\emph{pathwidth}
graphs -- the \emph{same} parameter TWIG's own DP is actually bounded by,
not the more general treewidth we originally framed it against
(Section~\ref{sec:twig-direction}): \texttt{ExactSpread}'s schedule is
strictly linear, with no join operation, so it cannot exploit tree
structure beyond a path decomposition either. Their abstract states that
their algorithm already achieves the same XP-to-FPT jump for the pathwidth
case via a construction in the spirit of
Theorem~\ref{thm:twig-collapse}'s collapse, so we do not claim that
theorem as a complexity advance over their result. What TWIG contributes
instead is a construction and correctness proof
(Theorem~\ref{thm:twig-correct}) checked against exhaustive brute-force on
60 small instances (Section~\ref{sec:twig-empirical}); the observation
that processing \emph{direction} along a fixed order is itself a
first-class choice, with a worst-case separation
(Proposition~\ref{prop:direction}) not discussed in their paper; and an
end-to-end blocking-selection pipeline (\texttt{greedy\_exact},
\texttt{brute\_force\_optimal\_block}) that measures, for the first time
free of Monte-Carlo confounds, how far greedy IMIN selection departs from
the true optimum. Our pathwidth DP follows the classical bounded-treewidth
programming paradigm \citep{bodlaender1988,cygan2015}.

\textbf{Adaptive submodular and sequential decision problems.} Adaptive
variants of influence \emph{maximization} are well studied:
\citet{golovinkrause2011} give the general adaptive-submodularity
framework; \citet{seemansinger2013} apply two-stage adaptive seeding to
influence maximization; and \citet{tong2017} is itself an explicitly
adaptive maximization algorithm that re-plans as the network changes,
structurally similar to DEFER but for the maximization objective. For
stochastic monotone submodular \emph{maximization}, the adaptivity gap --
introduced by \citet{deangoemansvondrak2008} for the stochastic knapsack
problem -- is \emph{bounded} by a constant
\citep{asadpournazerzadeh2016}; Theorem~\ref{thm:adaptivity-gap} shows the
opposite for IMIN, an \emph{unbounded} gap, attributable to
$\FF(\cdot)$'s lack of supermodularity in $B$
\citep[established by][]{xie2023}. To our knowledge no prior
work formalizes an adaptive variant of IMIN, or proves a lemma of the
shape of Lemma~\ref{lem:defer-free} or an unbounded-adaptivity-gap result
of the shape of Theorem~\ref{thm:adaptivity-gap}, for this minimization
problem.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{sec:prelim}

\subsection{The independent cascade model}\label{sec:ic-model}

$G=(V,E)$ is a directed graph,
$n=|V|$, $m=|E|$, with edge probabilities $p:E\to(0,1]$. A live-edge
realization $\omega\in\{0,1\}^E$ is drawn with
$\Pr[\omega]=\prod_{e}p_e^{\omega_e}(1-p_e)^{1-\omega_e}$. For a seed set
$S\subseteq V$ and blocked set $B\subseteq V\setminus S$, write
$G_B=G[V\setminus B]$ and let $\Rspread(\omega,B)\subseteq V\setminus B$ be
the set of vertices reachable from $S$ using only live edges of $G_B$. The
\emph{expected spread} is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:spread}
\FF(B)\;=\;\mathbb E_\omega\bigl[\,|\Rspread(\omega,B)\setminus S|\,\bigr].
\end{equation}

\subsection{Problem formalization}\label{sec:formalization}

\begin{definition}[IMIN, one-shot]\label{def:imin}
Given $G$, $p$, seed set $S$, and budget $b\in\mathbb N$, the
\emph{influence minimization} (IMIN) problem is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:imin}
\min_{B\subseteq V\setminus S}\ \FF(B)
\qquad\text{subject to}\qquad
|B|\le b.
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

\noindent \citet{xie2023} show Problem~\eqref{eq:imin} is NP-hard and hard
to approximate within any constant factor unless P$=$NP, and that $\FF$ is
not supermodular in $B$ under the IC model, so the greedy
$(1-1/e)$-guarantee available for influence \emph{maximization}
\citep{kempe2003} does not transfer.

To formalize the adaptive variant, let a \emph{history} up to round $t$ be
$h_t=(N_0,N_1,\dots,N_t)$, the sequence of newly activated sets observed so
far (full-adoption feedback: which vertices activate each round, not which
live edges carried the activation). Let $\mathcal H$ denote the set of all
histories of any length.

\begin{definition}[Adaptive policy]\label{def:policy}
An \emph{adaptive blocking policy} is a function
$\pi:\mathcal H\to 2^V$ mapping each history $h_t$ to a set of
not-yet-activated, not-yet-blocked vertices to additionally block in round
$t+1$, subject to $\sum_{t}|\pi(h_t)|\le b$ over the run of the cascade.
Write $\sigma(\pi)$ for the (random) final spread beyond $S$ that results
from running the cascade under policy $\pi$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[IMIN, adaptive]\label{def:adaptive-imin}
Given $G$, $p$, $S$, and budget $b$, the \emph{adaptive} IMIN problem is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:adaptive-imin}
\min_{\pi}\ \mathbb E\bigl[\sigma(\pi)\bigr]
\qquad\text{subject to}\qquad
\textstyle\sum_t|\pi(h_t)|\le b\ \text{a.s.}
\end{equation}
A one-shot policy is the special case $\pi(h_0)=B$ for a fixed
$B$ with $|B|\le b$ and $\pi(h_t)=\varnothing$ for $t\ge1$, so
Problem~\eqref{eq:imin} is exactly Problem~\eqref{eq:adaptive-imin}
restricted to one-shot policies, and
$\min_\pi\mathbb E[\sigma(\pi)]\le\min_{|B|\le b}\FF(B)$ trivially, since
every one-shot policy is a feasible (adaptive) policy. The
\emph{adaptivity gap} of an instance is the ratio of the right-hand side to
the left-hand side of this inequality; Theorem~\ref{thm:adaptivity-gap}
shows this gap is unbounded.
\end{definition}

DEFER (Section~\ref{sec:defer}) is a construction of a specific policy
$\pi_{\mathrm{DEFER}}$, parameterized by a choice of one-shot planner
$\mathcal P$, that is feasible for Problem~\eqref{eq:adaptive-imin} and
provably close to optimal relative to $\mathcal P$'s own one-shot value
(Theorem~\ref{thm:defer-dominates}). TWIG (Section~\ref{sec:twig}) instead
addresses an orthogonal computational issue in Problem~\eqref{eq:imin}
itself: $\FF(B)$, the objective being optimized, is a \#P-hard quantity to
evaluate exactly \citep{valiant1979,provan1983}, so every algorithm for
either problem above that a practitioner actually runs replaces $\FF(B)$
with a Monte-Carlo estimate; TWIG computes $\FF(B)$ exactly instead, on
graphs where doing so is tractable, to certify how much error that
replacement introduces.

\subsection{Pathwidth machinery}\label{sec:treewidth-prelim}

Used only in Section~\ref{sec:twig}. Fix a
permutation $\sigma=(\sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_n)$ of $V$ and write
$\mathrm{pos}(v)$ for $v$'s index. For $v=\sigma_i$, let
$\mathrm{connect\_to}(v)=\{u\in N(v):\mathrm{pos}(u)<i\}$ and
$\mathrm{last}(v)=\max_{x\in N[v]}\mathrm{pos}(x)$. The \emph{frontier}
after step $i$ is $F_i=\{\sigma_j:j\le i,\ \mathrm{last}(\sigma_j)\ge i\}$,
and the \emph{width} of $\sigma$ is $w=\max_i|F_i|-1$: the \emph{vertex
separation number} of $\sigma$. Minimizing $w$ over all orders $\sigma$
gives exactly the \emph{pathwidth} of $G$ \citep{kinnersley1992}, not its
treewidth: $w$ is always an upper bound on treewidth (any elimination
order's width bounds the width of the chordal completion it induces
\citep{bodlaender1988,cygan2015}), but the bound can be arbitrarily loose,
since pathwidth itself has no bound in terms of treewidth alone (a graph
of treewidth $1$ can have pathwidth $\Theta(\log n)$;
Section~\ref{sec:twig-direction}). A min-fill-in / min-degree chordal
completion heuristic \citep{bodlaender1988,cygan2015} gives a good
\emph{starting order} in practice, but the $w$ it realizes should be read
as this order's own width, not as an estimate of $G$'s treewidth.

\section{DEFER: Adaptive Influence Minimization by Deferred Frontier Blocking}\label{sec:defer}

\subsection{Algorithm}\label{sec:defer-algo}

DEFER wraps a one-shot planner $\mathcal P$ (any IMIN algorithm; by default
a dominator-tree greedy planner with isolation moves). At each round it
re-plans on the currently reachable region, keeps whichever of the new plan
and the previous round's uncommitted leftover looks better on freshly drawn
samples (\emph{plan protection}), and commits only the fraction of the plan
that is both exposed to the current frontier and passes a push-down test.

\begin{algorithm}[h]
\caption{DEFER}\label{alg:defer}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require graph $G$, probabilities $p$, seeds $S$, budget $b$, sample size
  $\theta$, one-shot planner $\mathcal P$
\State $A_0\gets S$, $B_0\gets\varnothing$, $F_0\gets S$, $L\gets\varnothing$ (uncommitted leftover)
\For{$t=0,1,2,\dots$ while the cascade is alive and $|B_t|<b$}
  \State $b_t\gets b-|B_t|$
  \State draw $\theta$ live-edge subgraphs reachable from $F_t$ avoiding $A_t\cup B_t$
  \State $P'\gets\mathcal P(\text{seeds}=F_t,\ \text{forbidden}=A_t\cup B_t,\ \text{budget}=b_t)$ on the samples
  \State draw a fresh, held-out set of $\theta$ live-edge subgraphs (disjoint from those used to compute $P'$)
  \State $\hat d\gets$ sample-average saving of $P'$ minus sample-average saving of $L$ on the held-out draws; $\widehat{\mathrm{se}}\gets$ their standard error
  \If{$\hat d>\widehat{\mathrm{se}}$} \Comment{plan protection: swap only if the improvement exceeds one held-out standard error}
    \State $P_t\gets P'$
  \Else
    \State $P_t\gets L$
  \EndIf
  \State $X_t\gets$ inactive, unblocked out-neighbors of $F_t$
  \State $\lambda_t\gets$ smallest marginal saving among the picks of $P_t$ (0 if $P_t$ does not exhaust $b_t$)
  \For{$v\in P_t\cap X_t$}\label{alg:pushdown-start}
    \State $q_v\gets 1-\prod_{u\in F_t}(1-p(u,v))$; $c_v\gets$ inactive unblocked out-degree of $v$
    \If{$\lambda_t=0$ \textbf{or} $q_v\,(c_v+1/\lambda_t)\ge 1$} \Comment{push-down rule, Lemma~\ref{lem:pushdown} (myopic, no budget-contention guarantee -- Remark~\ref{rem:pushdown-counterexample})}
      \State block $v$ now
    \EndIf
  \EndFor\label{alg:pushdown-end}
  \State $L\gets P_t\setminus(\text{blocked this round})$
  \State observe $N_{t+1}$; update $A_{t+1},F_{t+1}$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The push-down rule compares spending one budget unit on an exposed node $v$
now against waiting one round and, if $v$ activates (probability $q_v$),
protecting its $c_v$ children instead while losing $v$ itself. When the
planner is deterministic given the samples and no new information arrives,
DEFER reduces to lazily committing the one-shot planner's own plan
(Lemma~\ref{lem:defer-free}); the two ablations used in
Section~\ref{sec:experiments} are COMMIT (same loop, but the whole plan is
committed every round) and the one-shot planners run without any wrapper.

paragraph and Algorithm~\ref{alg:defer} describe.

\subsection{Worked example}\label{sec:defer-example}

We make the mechanism behind Theorem~\ref{thm:adaptivity-gap} concrete on
the star-of-paths instance its proof constructs
(Figure~\ref{fig:star-of-paths}), with $\Delta=4$ branches,
per-branch activation probability $p=0.1$, path length $L=50$, and budget
$b=1$. Seed $s$ has four out-neighbors $a_1,\dots,a_4$, each independently
live with probability $p$; each $a_i$ that activates starts a private,
deterministic path of $L=50$ further vertices. With no intervention, each
branch contributes $L+1=51$ vertices to the spread with probability $p$,
so the expected total spread is $\Delta p(L+1)=4(0.1)(51)=20.4$.

A one-shot policy with budget 1 must pick a single vertex to remove before
observing anything; by symmetry the best it can do is remove one branch's
gateway $a_i$ outright, which pre-empts that branch's entire contribution
regardless of whether $(s,a_i)$ would have been live. Its expected spread
is then the sum over the remaining three branches,
$(\Delta-1)p(L+1)=3(0.1)(51)=15.3$.

DEFER instead waits one round. It observes $N_1=\{a_i:(s,a_i)\text{ live}\}$
-- the number of active branches $k$ is $\mathrm{Binomial}(4,0.1)$, so
$k=0$ with probability $0.656$, $k=1$ with probability $0.292$, $k=2$ with
probability $0.049$, and $k\ge3$ with probability $0.004$ -- and spends its
one block on the first path vertex of one active branch (blocking $a_i$
itself is no longer possible once it has activated). This seals that one
branch's continuation at a cost of only the vertex $a_i$ already reached,
while $k-1$ other active branches, if any, still propagate their full
length-$L$ path. DEFER's expected spread is
$\Delta p+L\cdot\mathbb E[(k-1)^+]=0.4+50(0.0561)=3.205$, using
$\mathbb E[(k-1)^+]=\sum_{k\ge1}(k-1)\Pr[k]=0.0561$ computed from the
binomial probabilities above. Theorem~\ref{thm:adaptivity-gap} makes two
separate claims about this instance, and it is worth checking both
numerically. The \emph{saving} each policy achieves relative to no
intervention is $p(L+1)=0.1(51)=5.1$ for one-shot and
$L(1-(1-p)^\Delta)=50(1-0.9^4)=17.195$ for DEFER, a ratio of
$17.195/5.1\approx3.372$ -- below the limiting bound
$(1-(1-p)^\Delta)/p\approx3.439$ by exactly the factor $L/(L+1)=50/51$ the
theorem predicts, and approaching it as $L$ grows. The ratio of the two
policies' \emph{spreads} themselves is a different quantity,
$15.3/3.205\approx4.77$ here, and it is not bounded by $3.439$ as $p$
shrinks: unlike the saving ratio, which depends only on $\Delta$ and $p$,
the spread ratio needs $L$ to grow along with $1/p$ to reach its own
$L\to\infty$ limit $2/(\Delta p)$, which is unbounded as $p\to0$ at fixed
$\Delta$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:adaptivity-gap}'s proof verifies this
numerically at $\Delta=4$, $p=0.01$, $L=5000$: spread ratio $\approx49.7$,
close to $2/(\Delta p)=50$).

The qualitative point survives far more general instances: a one-shot
policy pays for protection whether or not the threat it protects against
ever materializes, while DEFER pays only for branches actually observed
live. Push-down (Lemma~\ref{lem:pushdown}) tells DEFER \emph{when} this is
worth it -- here, $c_v=1$, $q_v=p=0.1$ is small relative to the shadow
price $\lambda\approx pL\approx5$, so $q_v(c_v+1/\lambda)<1$ holds and
every exposed branch vertex is deferred; with $q_v$ near $1$ (a
near-certain threat) the same rule commits right away instead, recovering
one-shot behavior exactly where deferring would not help.

\subsection{Theoretical guarantees}\label{sec:defer-theory}

Full proofs of every result in this section are given in
Online Supplement~\ref{ec:defer-proofs}.

\begin{lemma}[Deferral is free]\label{lem:defer-free}
Fix a live-edge realization, the seeds, and any set $P$ with $|P|\le b$. Let
$\pi_P$ be the policy that, in every round, blocks the not-yet-blocked
vertices of $P$ that are exposed to the current frontier. Then $\pi_P$
produces exactly the same final active set as blocking $P$ at time $0$, and
blocks at most as many vertices.
\end{lemma}

\begin{theorem}[\texttt{DEFER-nopush} weakly dominates its planner]\label{thm:defer-dominates}
Let $P_0$ be $\mathcal P$'s plan at round $0$, and consider Algorithm~\ref{alg:defer}
with the push-down step (lines~\ref{alg:pushdown-start}--\ref{alg:pushdown-end})
removed, so every exposed planned vertex is committed immediately
(\texttt{DEFER-nopush}). If, in every later round, the plan-protection test
only ever replaces the leftover $L=P_{t-1}\setminus C_{t-1}$ by a new plan
$P'$ whose held-out sample-average saving exceeds $L$'s by more than one
held-out standard error, then the expected final spread of
\texttt{DEFER-nopush} is at most that of the one-shot solution $P_0$, up to
the residual error of that one-sided test (Remark~\ref{rem:winners-curse}).
\end{theorem}

\begin{remark}[The 1-SE margin bounds, but does not eliminate, selection
bias]\label{rem:winners-curse}
Choosing between two noisy sample averages risks the ``winner's curse'':
even equally-good $L,P'$ swap whenever $P'$'s held-out average exceeds
$L$'s by chance. The held-out draw removes same-data optimizing-and-testing
bias, and the margin makes an accidental swap less likely than a bare
$\arg\max$, but neither eliminates the residual chance a worse plan clears
it. We do not bound the resulting regret; Section~\ref{sec:experiments}'s
footnoted cells are candidate instances, not proof either way.
\end{remark}

\begin{lemma}[Push-down is the local myopic optimum]\label{lem:pushdown}
Consider an exposed planned vertex $v$ whose children (inactive
out-neighbors) jointly protect the same set as $v$ minus $v$ itself, assume
the children are exposed only through $v$, and value a budget unit at
$\lambda$ vertices. Among the two policies ``block $v$ now'' and ``block
$v$'s children if and only if $v$ activates,'' the second has smaller
expected loss (vertices lost plus $\lambda$ times budget spent) if and only
if $q_v(c_v+1/\lambda)<1$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{remark}[Push-down does not preserve
domination]\label{rem:pushdown-counterexample}
Lemma~\ref{lem:pushdown} is a purely local, single-vertex statement: it
prices the budget unit freed by deferring $v$ at a fixed $\lambda$,
available whenever needed, and says nothing about \emph{budget contention}
between several vertices deferred at once. When more branches activate
than the remaining budget can cover, push-down can leave full DEFER
strictly worse than the plan it wraps -- so Theorem~\ref{thm:defer-dominates}
does \emph{not} extend from \texttt{DEFER-nopush} to full DEFER. On the
star-of-paths instance of Section~\ref{sec:defer-example} with $\Delta=2$,
$p=0.4$, $L=1$, $b=1$: at round $0$ the myopic condition
$q_v(c_v+1/\lambda_t)<1$ holds for the planned gateway
($0.4(1+1/0.8)=0.9<1$), so push-down defers it, and by symmetry the same
would hold for the other gateway if budget allowed planning it too. If
both gateways activate (probability $p^2=0.16$), the single remaining
budget unit can protect only one child, and DEFER's expected spread is
$\mathbb E[\Delta p+L(k-1)^+]=0.96$ against the one-shot plan's $0.8$
-- DEFER strictly \emph{worse} than \texttt{AG} on this instance. We
therefore present Lemma~\ref{lem:pushdown} as a local heuristic
justification for push-down, not a global guarantee, and report
\texttt{DEFER-nopush} alongside full DEFER in
Section~\ref{sec:defer-experiments} so the push-down rule's empirical
effect -- small, and occasionally negative, e.g.\ on ca-GrQc under the
weighted-cascade model -- can be judged directly against
Theorem~\ref{thm:defer-dominates}'s guaranteed variant.
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}[The adaptivity gap of IMIN is unbounded]\label{thm:adaptivity-gap}
For every $\Delta\ge 2$, $p\in(0,1)$, and path length $L\ge1$ there is an
instance with maximum out-degree $\Delta$, budget $1$, on which the best
one-shot solution and DEFER (Section~\ref{sec:defer-example} works through
this instance in full, including the push-down decision) have expected
\emph{savings} (spread reduction relative to no intervention) $p(L+1)$ and
$L\bigl(1-(1-p)^\Delta\bigr)$ respectively, so DEFER's saving
is $\frac{L}{L+1}\cdot\frac{1-(1-p)^\Delta}{p}$ times the one-shot saving --
a factor increasing in $L$ to the limit $\bigl(1-(1-p)^\Delta\bigr)/p$,
which in turn tends to $\Delta$ as $p\to0$; hence this saving ratio is
unbounded as $\Delta\to\infty$. The ratio of the two policies' expected
\emph{spreads} themselves is a different quantity, generally smaller than
the saving ratio at any finite $L$: it is \emph{strictly less than}
$\frac{(\Delta-1)p}{\Delta p-1+(1-p)^\Delta}$ for every finite $L$,
increasing monotonically in $L$ toward this value as $L\to\infty$ (it never
reaches it at finite $L$, so this quantity is a supremum, not a lower
bound, on the finite-$L$ spread ratio). Sending $L\to\infty$ first and then
$p\to0$, for \emph{fixed} $\Delta\ge2$, this limiting value itself diverges
at rate $2/(\Delta p)$; consequently the spread ratio can be made
arbitrarily large already at a fixed, finite $\Delta$, provided $L$ is
taken large enough relative to $1/p$ -- unboundedness here genuinely
requires $L$ to grow together with $1/p$, since for any \emph{fixed} $L$
the spread ratio instead stays bounded as $p\to0$ (it tends to the finite
constant $(\Delta-1)(L+1)/\Delta$).
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Complexity of DEFER]\label{thm:defer-complexity}
Let $n_t,m_t$ be the number of vertices and edges reachable from $F_t$ in
the union of the $\theta$ samples, $r$ the number of rounds, and $\alpha$
the inverse Ackermann function. With the dominator planner, round $t$ costs
$O(\theta\,m_t\,\alpha(m_t,n_t))$ for the dominator trees plus
$O(b_t\,\theta'\,m_t\,\alpha)$ for the greedy picks ($\theta'\le\theta$);
the total is $O\bigl(\sum_t(1+b_t)\,\theta\,m_t\,\alpha\bigr)$, which is
$O(r\,b\,\theta\,m\,\alpha(m,n))$ in the worst case.
\end{theorem}

We additionally construct \texttt{isocut}, a one-shot planner of our own
that adds one further kind of candidate move to AG's planner --
isolating a whole seed's out-neighborhood at once, rather than blocking
one vertex at a time -- and prove it admits a worst-case separation
(Theorem~\ref{thm:isocut-separation}, Online Supplement) from published
one-shot planners: AdvancedGreedy, GreedyReplace, and the Sandwich lower
bound. \texttt{isocut} is not itself a competing baseline from the
literature; it is reported only as an ablation, in
Online Supplement~\ref{sec:isocut-practice}, because it is rarely selected
on the SNAP graphs used there.

\begin{theorem}[Isolation-move separation]\label{thm:isocut-separation}
There is a family of instances on which AdvancedGreedy, GreedyReplace, and
the Sandwich lower-bound greedy all save $O(b)$ vertices with budget $b$,
while a planner with frontier-isolation moves saves $\Omega(N)$ for
arbitrary $N$.
\end{theorem}

\section{TWIG: Exact Pathwidth-Bounded Validation}\label{sec:twig}

Every quantity reported by a one-shot IMIN heuristic -- including the plans
DEFER wraps -- is normally a Monte-Carlo estimate of Equation
\eqref{eq:spread}, because computing $\FF(B)$ exactly is a network-reliability
computation and is \#P-hard in general \citep{valiant1979,provan1983}. TWIG
computes $\FF(B)$ \emph{exactly} on graphs of bounded pathwidth (an elimination
order with small vertex-separation number; Section~\ref{sec:treewidth-prelim}),
giving a ground-truth oracle that we use to certify how close greedy
selection comes to the true optimum, free of sampling noise.

\subsection{Algorithm}\label{sec:twig-algo}

\begin{definition}[Configuration]\label{def:config}
A configuration over a vertex set $F\subseteq V$ is a pair
$\kappa=(\Pi,\rho)$ where $\Pi$ is a set partition of $F$ with at most one
distinguished block $\mathrm{SEED}$, and $\rho$ maps each non-$\mathrm{SEED}$
block to a nonnegative integer (its \emph{pending count}).
\end{definition}

\texttt{ExactSpread} maintains, after each step $i$ of the schedule defined
in Section~\ref{sec:prelim}, a probability distribution over configurations
on the frontier $F_i$, plus a running scalar $\mathrm{acc}_i$, updated by
three operations: \emph{introduce} a new singleton block for each newly
processed vertex; \emph{process\_edge} branches on each edge's live/dead
outcome, merging blocks on the live branch and paying out to $\mathrm{acc}$
when a merge is into $\mathrm{SEED}$; and \emph{retire} pays out $\mathrm{acc}$
for a retiring $\mathrm{SEED}$-block vertex (unless it is itself a seed), or
defers a retiring ordinary vertex into its block's pending count, discarding
that count once the block empties of active members.

\subsection{Worked example}\label{sec:twig-example}

We trace \texttt{ExactSpread} on the smallest instance that already
exercises every case of Definition~\ref{def:config}: the path
$1$--$2$--$3$--$4$, seed $S=\{1\}$, no blocking ($B=\varnothing$), and edge
probabilities $p_{12}=p_{23}=p_{34}=0.5$. The elimination order is the
natural order $1,2,3,4$ (a path has treewidth 1 and either direction keeps
the frontier at size $\le2$). Directly, $F(\varnothing)=\Pr[2\text{
reached}]+\Pr[3\text{ reached}]+\Pr[4\text{ reached}]
=p_{12}+p_{12}p_{23}+p_{12}p_{23}p_{34}=0.5+0.25+0.125=0.875$.

\texttt{ExactSpread} reaches the same number without ever conditioning on
all $2^3=8$ live-edge histories explicitly (Figure~\ref{fig:twig-path-example}).
After introducing vertices 1 and 2, \emph{process\_edge}(1,2) branches: w.p.\
$0.5$ nothing changes; w.p.\ $0.5$ vertex 2 merges into SEED, paying out
$0$ (no previously-retired vertex was waiting on this block). Vertex 1
retires excluded from payout (it is a seed), leaving two configurations:
$\{2\}$ ordinary or $\{2\}\equiv\mathrm{SEED}$, each probability $0.5$. The
same branching repeats at vertices 3 and 4. Vertex 2 retires at step 2: in
the SEED configurations (combined probability $0.5$, from $(1,2)$ live),
the payout rule contributes $\mathrm{acc}\mathrel{+}=0.5=p_{12}$; in the
other two, its ordinary block merges into a later live edge or is
discarded with no payout, correctly reflecting that a component
disconnected from the seed is never reached. Table~\ref{tab:twig-example}
lists all eight histories grouped by configuration; summing $P_\kappa$
over SEED-at-retirement histories reproduces
$\Pr[2\text{ reached}]=0.5$, $\Pr[3\text{ reached}]=0.25$,
$\Pr[4\text{ reached}]=0.125$, totaling $0.875=F(\varnothing)$, matching
Theorem~\ref{thm:twig-correct}.

\begin{theorem}[Correctness of \texttt{ExactSpread}]\label{thm:twig-correct}

\end{theorem}

\subsection{Complexity: from XP to FPT in the width $w$}\label{sec:twig-complexity}

\begin{proposition}[State space of \texttt{ExactSpread}]\label{prop:xp-space}
The number of distinct configurations at any step is at most
$B_{w+2}\cdot(n+1)^{w+1}$, where $B_{w+2}$ is the $(w+2)$-th Bell number: a
frontier of size $m\le w+1$ has $B_m$ partitions, and marking one block as
$\mathrm{SEED}$ (or none) turns each into $\mathrm{blocks}(\Pi)+1$
configurations, summing over all partitions to $B_{m+1}\le B_{w+2}$.
\end{proposition}

This makes \texttt{ExactSpread} XP, not FPT, in $w$: the degree of the
polynomial in $n$ grows with $w$.

\begin{theorem}[Correctness of the linear-aggregation collapse]\label{thm:twig-collapse}
Fix a partition shape $s$ and, at some step, let
$\mathcal K_s=\{(s,\rho):P_{(s,\rho)}>0\}$. Define the aggregates
$P_s=\sum_{(s,\rho)\in\mathcal K_s}P_{(s,\rho)}$ and
$W_b=\sum_{(s,\rho)\in\mathcal K_s}P_{(s,\rho)}\cdot\rho(b)$ for each
non-$\mathrm{SEED}$ block $b$ of $s$. Then $(P_s,\{W_b\})_s$ can be updated
at every step by the four rules \texttt{ExactSpreadFast} implements, and
this update yields the identical sequence of $\mathrm{acc}$ increments as
\texttt{ExactSpread}, for every $B$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proposition}[State space of \texttt{ExactSpreadFast}]\label{prop:fpt-space}
The number of distinct states at any step is at most $B_{w+2}$, independent
of $n$, giving overall running time $O(n\cdot B_{w+2}\cdot\mathrm{poly}(w))$.
\end{proposition}

Proposition~\ref{prop:fpt-space} is a genuine change of complexity class
from Proposition~\ref{prop:xp-space}, not merely a constant-factor speed-up:
the $n^{w+1}$ factor is eliminated entirely.

\subsection{Processing direction is a first-class algorithmic choice}\label{sec:twig-direction}

\begin{proposition}[Star graphs separate forward and reversed elimination order]\label{prop:direction}
Let $T_k$ be the star $K_{1,k}$, $n=k+1$. Min-fill-in computes the
elimination order $\pi=(\ell_1,\dots,\ell_k,c)$, correctly certifying
treewidth $1$. Running the schedule of Section~\ref{sec:prelim} directly on
$\pi$ (the centre processed last) produces a frontier of size $k=n-1$
immediately before the centre's step. Running it on $\mathrm{reverse}(\pi)$
(the centre processed first) keeps the frontier at size at most $2$
throughout.
\end{proposition}

This is a worst-case separation on an exact, checkable family: a
bounded-treewidth graph (here, treewidth $1$, for any $n$) on which the
\emph{same} elimination order, run in the wrong direction, forces the true
state space of the dynamic program to scale with $n$ instead of remaining
bounded by a function of the width alone. In our implementation the
elimination order is therefore always reversed before scheduling.

Reversal fixes the star, but not every bounded-treewidth graph:
\texttt{ExactSpread}'s schedule is strictly \emph{linear} (no operation
splits into independently-scheduled subproblems and rejoins them, as a
genuine tree-decomposition DP would), so the width $w$ it realizes is
exactly the \emph{vertex separation number} of the order, and its minimum
over all orders is, classically, the graph's \emph{pathwidth}
\citep{kinnersley1992} -- unbounded in terms of treewidth alone. A
complete binary tree of height $h$ has treewidth $1$ but pathwidth
$\Theta(h)$ \citep{kinnersley1992}: running this implementation's actual
order-and-schedule (min-fill-in, best of forward/reversed) on heights
$2$--$7$ gives frontier peaks $2,3,4,5,6,7$, growing with $h$ exactly as
predicted while treewidth stays $1$. \emph{TWIG is therefore a
bounded-\underline{pathwidth} algorithm, not a bounded-treewidth one}:
Propositions~\ref{prop:xp-space}--\ref{prop:fpt-space}'s bounds are in
$w$, which can exceed treewidth without bound on tree-like inputs. The
algorithm remains exact and polynomial for any fixed $w$; it just scales,
in practice, to graphs of small pathwidth (dense, non-tree-like small
networks such as Table~\ref{tab:twig-feasibility}'s), not merely small
treewidth.

processing order of Proposition~\ref{prop:direction} makes exact evaluation
tractable on real, non-synthetic small networks:

On the karate club network (one seed, random independent-cascade
probabilities in $[0.05,0.4]$), \texttt{greedy\_exact} -- greedy selection
using \texttt{ExactSpreadFast} itself as the marginal-gain oracle, with zero
sampling noise -- selects node 2 at budget 1 (expected spread falls from the
unblocked value to 7.98) and adds node 1 at budget 2 (expected spread
5.51). \citet{xie2023} prove $\FF(\cdot)$ is not supermodular in $B$ under
the independent-cascade model, so greedy carries no $(1-1/e)$-style
guarantee for IMIN, and \citet{xie2024} further show IMIN is APX-hard;
consistent with both results, comparing \texttt{greedy\_exact} against
\texttt{brute\_force\_optimal\_block} on 60 small random instances
($n=6$--$14$, budget 1--4) finds greedy exactly optimal in 56 of 60 and
strictly suboptimal in 4, by up to 84.6\% relative excess spread in the
worst case (mean excess 2.2\% across all 60). This is, to our knowledge, the
first exact (not Monte-Carlo-confounded) measurement of how often and how
badly greedy departs from optimal IMIN selection.

\subsection{Complexity comparison}\label{sec:complexity-comparison}

Table~\ref{tab:complexity} collects the time complexity of every method
this paper compares against or introduces, so the cost of DEFER's
adaptivity and of TWIG's exactness can be read off against the published
one-shot planners in one place. Three points are worth stating explicitly.
First, DEFER's cost (Theorem~\ref{thm:defer-complexity}) is exactly AG's
per-round cost multiplied by the number of rounds $r$: adaptivity is not
free computationally, even though Theorem~\ref{thm:defer-dominates} shows
it is free in expected-spread terms. Second, TWIG's two variants solve a
strictly harder problem than AG/GR/DEFER (exact evaluation of $\FF(B)$
rather than an unbiased Monte-Carlo estimate of it) and are not drop-in
replacements for the sampling-based planners at the graph sizes those
planners target; Proposition~\ref{prop:fpt-space}'s
$n$-independence is what makes \texttt{ExactSpreadFast} usable at all
compared to the brute-force alternative of enumerating all $2^m$ live-edge
realizations, but $B_{w+2}$ still grows very quickly in the width $w$
(Table~\ref{tab:bell-numbers}). Third, \texttt{brute\_force\_optimal\_block}
compounds TWIG's per-evaluation cost with an outer search over all
size-$\le b$ subsets, which is what confines
Section~\ref{sec:twig-empirical}'s optimality-gap study to $n\le14$.

\textbf{Ranking, and whether the experiments confirm it.} Among the four
methods benchmarked empirically in Section~\ref{sec:defer-experiments}
(AG, GR, DEFER, COMMIT; \texttt{isocut} is asymptotically identical to AG),
the smallest cost by Table~\ref{tab:complexity} is AG's, since GR differs
only by an $O(1)$-factor replacement pass on top of the same single
planning call. DEFER and COMMIT are the largest, both scaling as
$O(r\,b\,\theta\,m\,\alpha)$ with the number of rounds $r$ rather than a
single call; the theory does not rank DEFER against COMMIT a priori,
because $r$ is realized by the cascade, not fixed by the algorithm.
Online Supplement Table~\ref{tab:timing}'s wall-clock measurements confirm
exactly this shape: AG is cheapest on every one of the four networks, GR
is a small constant factor above AG (roughly 1.1--1.3$\times$), and DEFER
and COMMIT are both several times to an order of magnitude above both
(DEFER/AG ranges from about 2.5$\times$ on power-grid and BA(2000) to
about $4$--$7\times$ on ca-HepTh and ca-GrQc) -- but COMMIT, not DEFER, is
the most expensive throughout, which the complexity bound alone does not
predict and which Online Supplement~\ref{sec:complexity-practice}'s
explanation (COMMIT's worse plan quality realizes a larger $r$) accounts
for. On the TWIG side, Proposition~\ref{prop:fpt-space}'s removal of the
$(n+1)^{w+1}$ factor is not a paper claim left unchecked: Table
\ref{tab:twig-feasibility}'s own note reports that, before the collapse of
Theorem~\ref{thm:twig-collapse} and the reversed processing order of
Proposition~\ref{prop:direction} were both applied, the karate network
(the smallest of the three shown) did not finish evaluation in 60 seconds
under \texttt{ExactSpread} (XP), whereas \texttt{ExactSpreadFast} (FPT)
evaluates it in 2.5 seconds -- an empirical confirmation, not merely an
asymptotic one, that removing the $n$-dependent factor is what makes exact
evaluation of real (not synthetic) networks practical at all. Ordered from
smallest to largest measured cost throughout this paper:
\texttt{ExactSpreadFast} per call $<$ AG/GR/\texttt{isocut}
$\ll$ DEFER $<$ COMMIT $\ll$ \texttt{ExactSpread} per call (once $w$ is
large enough to matter) $\ll$ \texttt{greedy\_exact}
$\ll$ \texttt{brute\_force\_optimal\_block}.

\section{Experiments}\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{DEFER benchmark}\label{sec:defer-experiments}

To keep clear which methods are prior published work that DEFER is
compared \emph{against}, and which are our own constructions used to
isolate \emph{why} DEFER behaves as it does, we list the two groups
separately. Everything with ``DEFER'' or ``defer'' in its name below
belongs to the second group: it is a variant or ablation of our own
method, not a competing baseline.

\noindent\textbf{External one-shot baselines (prior work):}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{AdvancedGreedy (AG)} \citep{xie2024}: picks vertices
  greedily by dominator-tree subtree size on Monte-Carlo live-edge
  samples, recomputing after each pick; the one-shot planner DEFER wraps
  by default.
\item \textbf{GreedyReplace (GR)} \citep{xie2024}: AG followed by a
  replacement pass that swaps any picked vertex for a better one if doing
  so improves the sampled objective; the alternate planner DEFER can wrap
  (Section~\ref{sec:defer-algo}).
\item \textbf{Sandwich lower bound (LSBM)} \citep{wang2024sandwich}: a
  greedy maximum-coverage heuristic over dominator chains that gives a
  provable lower bound on the optimal one-shot solution.
\item \textbf{Proximity}: a simple one-shot heuristic that blocks the
  vertices closest to the seed set by hop distance; included as a
  na\"ive reference point, not a state-of-the-art baseline.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent\textbf{Our own ablations (all wrap or extend AG, not external
methods):}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{DEFER}: the contribution of this paper (Section~\ref{sec:defer-algo}),
  wrapping AG by default.
\item \textbf{\texttt{DEFER-nopush}}: DEFER with the push-down step
  (lines~\ref{alg:pushdown-start}--\ref{alg:pushdown-end} of
  Algorithm~\ref{alg:defer}) removed, so every exposed planned vertex is
  committed immediately; this is the variant Theorem~\ref{thm:defer-dominates}
  actually guarantees (Remark~\ref{rem:pushdown-counterexample}), reported
  alongside full DEFER to show push-down's empirical effect.
\item \textbf{COMMIT}: DEFER's loop with deferral itself removed --
  committing the entire plan immediately every round instead of only the
  exposed, push-down-cleared fraction; isolates the effect of re-planning
  alone from the effect of deferred commitment.
\item \textbf{isocut}: AG with one additional candidate move -- isolating
  a whole seed's out-neighborhood at once
  (Section~\ref{sec:complexity-comparison}, Theorem~\ref{thm:isocut-separation});
  a one-shot planner, not a DEFER variant, used only in
  Online Supplement~\ref{sec:isocut-practice}.
\item \textbf{defer\_cut}: DEFER wrapping \texttt{isocut} instead of plain
  AG, used only in Online Supplement~\ref{sec:isocut-practice} to check
  whether isocut's worst-case advantage survives once DEFER's adaptivity
  is layered on top.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent The networks are:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{ca-GrQc} and \textbf{ca-HepTh} \citep{leskovec2007}: arXiv
  co-authorship collaboration networks (General Relativity and High Energy
  Physics Theory categories respectively), $n=5241$ and $n=9875$.
\item \textbf{Power-grid} \citep{watts1998}: the topology of the Western
  US power grid, $n=4941$, previously used for epidemic-source inference
  by \citet{lokhov2014}.
\item \textbf{BA(2000)}: a synthetic Barab\'asi--Albert preferential-attachment
  graph \citep{barabasi1999} with $n=2000$ and attachment parameter $m=2$.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent We also attempted soc-Epinions1 (a much larger trust network),
which did not finish within budget and is left for future work. All four
networks are evaluated under constant activation probability and the
weighted-cascade (WC) model, seed sets of size 20 chosen uniformly at
random, budget 20 as the primary comparison point (the full sweep also
covers budgets 5--100, reported in
\texttt{results/summary\_adaptive.csv}). Table~\ref{tab:defer-results}

\subsection{Auxiliary benchmark: source detection}\label{sec:source-experiments}

As a secondary, apples-to-apples benchmark motivated by the same
misinformation/epidemic-control setting as DEFER and TWIG (identifying
where a cascade started, complementing where to block it from spreading
further, though not itself a contribution of this paper -- see
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}), we also evaluate thirteen classical and
learned source-detection methods, reproducing the methodology of
\citet{sterchi2026} on six real contact/collaboration networks. GNN-based
methods (GCN/GAT, IGCN) win on 4 of 5 fully-evaluated networks, and a
direct reproduction check against \citet{sterchi2026}'s own published
karate numbers matches to within 2.3 percentage points on 9 of 10 methods
(0.1--0.3 points for the two GNN architectures central to their headline
comparison). Full methods, networks, tables, and the reproduction check
are in Online Supplement~\ref{ec:source-experiments}.

\section{Discussion and Conclusion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{When does DEFER help in practice, and when is it unnecessary?}

Theorem~\ref{thm:adaptivity-gap} and Section~\ref{sec:defer-example}
locate the source of \texttt{DEFER-nopush}'s advantage precisely: it is
large when a vertex's protection is cheap to buy \emph{after} it is known
threatened but expensive \emph{unconditionally}, i.e.\ when activation
probabilities are low relative to the graph's branching factor. The
benchmark confirms this rather than merely asserting it: at budget $20$
under the constant-probability model, DEFER saves $0.2$--$1.9$ percentage
points more spread than AG on the four SNAP networks
(Table~\ref{tab:defer-results}), a margin that shrinks as the budget grows
(Figure~\ref{fig:defer-sweep}, Table~\ref{tab:defer-allbudgets}) and is
somewhat larger at small budgets ($0.2$--$3.9$ points over
$b\in\{5,10,20,50,100\}$), at roughly $2.5$--$7\times$ AG's per-episode
compute (Online Supplement Table~\ref{tab:timing});
under weighted cascade the margin is within the residual selection-bias
risk of Remark~\ref{rem:winners-curse}, so the extra compute buys little
there. A practitioner should ask whether the intervention has this shape
-- occasional high-fan-out gatekeepers, individually unlikely but
consequential -- rather than treat adaptivity as free and always better.
\texttt{DEFER-nopush} is never worse than its planner
(Theorem~\ref{thm:defer-dominates}); full DEFER is not
(Remark~\ref{rem:pushdown-counterexample}), though
Table~\ref{tab:defer-nopush-parity} in the Online Supplement shows the two
agree to within $0.0075$ of saved fraction on every benchmark cell we ran.
DEFER also assumes full-adoption feedback with negligible latency: the
controller must observe each round's activations before the next round's
propagation, realistic when suspensions or quarantines are fast relative
to the cascade's own timescale, but not otherwise; noisy, delayed, or
partially-observed feedback is open.

\subsection{When is TWIG the right tool?}\label{sec:discussion-twig}

TWIG is not a substitute for the Monte-Carlo-based evaluation AG, GR, and
DEFER rely on at SNAP scale (Section~\ref{sec:defer-experiments}):
Proposition~\ref{prop:fpt-space}'s $B_{w+2}$ state count is exponential in
the width $w$ regardless of $n$-independence, and
Table~\ref{tab:twig-feasibility}'s real networks already span tractable
(karate, $w=10$) to infeasible by memory (dolphin, $w=19$), two orders of
magnitude below ca-GrQc in $n$ alone. TWIG's role is instead as a
validation instrument, certifying exactly how far greedy departs from
optimal (Section~\ref{sec:twig-empirical}'s 60-instance study) -- greedy
under noisy estimates is a source of bias more sampling does not remove.
Validating greedy IMIN against TWIG before trusting it at scale is
analogous to unit-testing a numerical solver against closed-form
instances; three implementation bugs caught only by exhaustive
brute-force comparison show why that step is not optional.

\subsection{Limitations and Concluding Remarks}\label{sec:conclusion}

Two further limitations, beyond TWIG's scale limit already discussed
(Section~\ref{sec:discussion-twig}): Section~\ref{sec:defer-experiments}'s
benchmark covers four SNAP-scale networks with few live-edge draws per
cell, not a mature large-scale evaluation, though the theory does not
depend on this; and Section~\ref{sec:source-experiments}'s benchmark
reproduces a companion project problem, not a contribution of this paper.

\textbf{Code and data availability.} DEFER, TWIG, and every baseline and
benchmark script are released at
\url{https://github.com/vinhqdang/trace_e}. Networks used are either
generated by the released code (Barab\'asi--Albert, Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi) or
the small public datasets cited in
Section~\ref{sec:defer-experiments}; \texttt{results/summary\_adaptive.csv}
and the other \texttt{results/*.csv} files referenced throughout are the
raw per-instance logs the released benchmark scripts produce,
included in the same repository.

\section{Proofs for Section~\ref{sec:defer} (DEFER)}\label{ec:defer-proofs}

\begin{repeatlemma}[Lemma~\ref{lem:defer-free} (Deferral is free)]
Fix a live-edge realization, the seeds, and any set $P$ with $|P|\le b$. Let
$\pi_P$ be the policy that, in every round, blocks the not-yet-blocked
vertices of $P$ that are exposed to the current frontier. Then $\pi_P$
produces exactly the same final active set as blocking $P$ at time $0$, and
blocks at most as many vertices.
\end{repeatlemma}
\begin{proof}{Proof}
On a fixed realization a vertex $v$ becomes active in the first round in
which a frontier vertex has a live edge into it, i.e.\ the round after it is
first exposed. $\pi_P$ blocks $v\in P$ in that round, before its trial, so
$v$ never activates; every vertex outside $P$ faces the same set of blocked
vertices at each of its trials under both policies, because a vertex of $P$
that is never exposed never takes part in any trial. Hence the activation
sequences coincide. Vertices of $P$ that are never exposed are never
blocked.\Halmos
\end{proof}

\begin{repeattheorem}[Theorem~\ref{thm:defer-dominates} (\texttt{DEFER-nopush} weakly dominates its planner)]
Let $P_0$ be the plan of $\mathcal P$ at round $0$, and consider
Algorithm~\ref{alg:defer} with the push-down step removed
(\texttt{DEFER-nopush}: every exposed planned vertex is committed
immediately). If, in every later round, the plan-protection test only ever
replaces the leftover $L=P_{t-1}\setminus C_{t-1}$ by a new plan $P'$
whose held-out sample-average saving exceeds $L$'s by more than one
held-out standard error, then the expected final spread of
\texttt{DEFER-nopush} is at most that of the one-shot solution $P_0$, up to
the residual error of that one-sided test (Remark~\ref{rem:winners-curse}).
\end{repeattheorem}
\begin{proof}{Proof}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:defer-free} the policy that keeps $P_0$ and commits
lazily (blocking every not-yet-blocked vertex of $P_0$ the moment it is
exposed) has the same spread as blocking $P_0$ at time $0$.
\texttt{DEFER-nopush} differs from it only by replacing, at each round,
the uncommitted leftover by a plan that clears the plan-protection test
for the remaining budget, and then committing \emph{every} exposed vertex
of the resulting plan immediately (no push-down deferral); the committed
prefix is otherwise identical to lazily committing whichever plan is
currently standing. If every swap were only ever made when $P'$ is truly
at least as good as $L$, induction over rounds would give the claim
exactly, since replacing a standing plan by an at-least-as-good one and
then committing lazily can only weakly improve on committing the original
lazily (again by Lemma~\ref{lem:defer-free}, applied to whichever plan is
current at each round). The test instead accepts $P'$ whenever its
held-out sample-average saving exceeds $L$'s by more than one held-out
standard error -- a one-sided test against equally-good alternatives,
using concentration of the sample average (\citealp{xie2024}, applied per
candidate plan) on data disjoint from whatever $P'$ was optimized on, so
the comparison itself is not confounded by having been optimized and
tested on the same draws. This rules out the most severe form of
optimizer's-curse bias (comparing a plan against the very data used to
select it) but not the residual chance that a $P'$ that is not truly
better than $L$ still clears the $1$-SE margin on a given round's held-out
draw; Remark~\ref{rem:winners-curse} discusses this residual, which we do
not bound here. Push-down is excluded from this argument entirely: it can
permanently forfeit protection of a deferred vertex if the budget that was
supposed to remain available for it is spent elsewhere first, which is
exactly the failure mode Remark~\ref{rem:pushdown-counterexample}
exhibits.\Halmos
\end{proof}

\begin{repeatremark}[Remark~\ref{rem:winners-curse} (The 1-SE margin bounds, but does not eliminate, selection bias)]
Choosing between two noisy sample averages is a textbook source of
upward-biased apparent improvement (the ``winner's curse''): if $L$ and
$P'$ were in fact equally good, the test still swaps to $P'$ whenever its
held-out average happens to exceed $L$'s by more than one standard error,
which is not a negligible event. Requiring a fresh, held-out draw for the
comparison removes the optimistic bias of optimizing and testing on the
same data, and the $1$-standard-error margin makes an accidental swap less
likely than a bare $\arg\max$ would; neither removes the residual chance
that a truly worse plan clears the margin on a given round's held-out
draw. We do not prove a bound on the resulting regret here.
\end{repeatremark}

\begin{repeatlemma}[Lemma~\ref{lem:pushdown} (Push-down is the local myopic optimum)]
Consider an exposed planned vertex $v$ whose children (its inactive
out-neighbors) jointly protect the same set as $v$ minus $v$ itself, assume
the children are exposed only through $v$, and value a budget unit at
$\lambda$ vertices. Among the two policies ``block $v$ now'' and ``block
$v$'s children if and only if $v$ activates,'' the second has smaller
expected loss (vertices lost plus $\lambda$ times budget spent) if and only
if $q_v(c_v+1/\lambda)<1$.
\end{repeatlemma}
\begin{proof}{Proof}
Blocking now costs $\lambda$ and loses nothing of $v$'s set. Waiting loses
$v$ (one vertex) and spends $c_v$ units with probability $q_v$, and nothing
otherwise, while the protected set is the same in both branches by
assumption: expected loss $q_v+\lambda q_v c_v$. Comparing $\lambda$ against
$q_v+\lambda q_v c_v$ gives the stated condition. Note that $\lambda$ is
treated as a fixed price at which a budget unit can always be redeployed;
the lemma says nothing about what happens when several vertices are
deferred under this reasoning simultaneously and the budget they were all
counting on cannot cover all of them at once
(Remark~\ref{rem:pushdown-counterexample}).\Halmos
\end{proof}

\begin{repeatremark}[Remark~\ref{rem:pushdown-counterexample} (Push-down does not preserve domination)]
On the star-of-paths instance with $\Delta=2$, $p=0.4$, $L=1$, $b=1$
(Section~\ref{sec:defer-example}, Remark~\ref{rem:pushdown-counterexample}
give the setup and the headline numbers, $0.96$ vs.\ $0.8$), the
case-by-case detail behind that expected spread is: with
$k\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(2,p)$ gateways active, $k\le1$ contributes spread
$k$ (the one active branch, if any, is sealed at the cost of the vertex
already reached), while $k=2$ (probability $p^2=0.16$) leaves only one
budget unit for two simultaneously-deferred children, so one branch's
path vertex activates unblocked, contributing spread $2+L=3$. Summing,
$\Pr[k{=}0]\cdot0+\Pr[k{=}1]\cdot1+\Pr[k{=}2]\cdot3=0.36(0)+0.48(1)+0.16(3)=0.96$.
\end{repeatremark}

\begin{repeattheorem}[Theorem~\ref{thm:adaptivity-gap} (The adaptivity gap of IMIN is unbounded)]
For every $\Delta\ge 2$, $p\in(0,1)$, and path length $L\ge1$ there is an
instance with maximum out-degree $\Delta$, budget $1$, on which the best
one-shot solution and DEFER have expected savings $p(L+1)$ and
$L(1-(1-p)^\Delta)$ respectively, so DEFER's saving is
$\frac{L}{L+1}\cdot\frac{1-(1-p)^\Delta}{p}$ times the one-shot saving, a
factor tending to $(1-(1-p)^\Delta)/p$ (which itself tends to $\Delta$ as
$p\to0$) as $L\to\infty$; the ratio of the two policies' expected spreads
is, at every finite $L$, strictly \emph{less} than
$\frac{(\Delta-1)p}{\Delta p-1+(1-p)^\Delta}$, increasing to this value only
in the limit $L\to\infty$, which (taken before $p\to0$, for fixed $\Delta$)
diverges.
\end{repeattheorem}
\begin{proof}{Proof}
Seed $s$ has out-neighbors $a_1,\dots,a_\Delta$ with $p(s,a_i)=p$; each
$a_i$ starts a private directed path of length $L$ with edge probability
$1$. Any one-shot solution with budget $1$ blocks one vertex; blocking
$a_i$ (or a vertex on its path) saves exactly $L+1$ vertices if $a_i$ is
activated, which happens with probability $p$, and nothing otherwise, so
its expected saving is $p(L+1)$ and its expected spread is
$(\Delta-1)p(L+1)$ (the other $\Delta-1$ branches are unprotected). DEFER
observes $N_1=\{a_i:(s,a_i)\text{ live}\}$ and blocks the first path vertex
of one activated branch (its plan at round $0$ is a branch head $a_i$ with
$q=p$, $c=1$, and $\lambda\approx pL$, so the push-down rule
$p(1+1/(pL))<1$ defers it); if $k$ branches are active the spread is
$(k-1)^+L+k$. Its expected saving is $L\Pr[k\ge1]=L(1-(1-p)^\Delta)$ (one
branch's continuation is sealed for the cost of the vertex already reached,
regardless of $k$) and its expected spread is
$\mathbb E[(k-1)^+]L+\Delta p=(\Delta p-1+(1-p)^\Delta)L+\Delta p$, using
$\mathbb E[(k-1)^+]=\mathbb E[k]-\Pr[k\ge1]=\Delta p-(1-(1-p)^\Delta)$.

Two distinct ratios follow from these exact expressions. The \emph{saving}
ratio is $\frac{L(1-(1-p)^\Delta)}{p(L+1)}=\frac{L}{L+1}\cdot
\frac{1-(1-p)^\Delta}{p}$, increasing in $L$ to the limit
$(1-(1-p)^\Delta)/p$, which tends to $\Delta$ as $p\to0$ (since
$1-(1-p)^\Delta=\Delta p-\binom\Delta2p^2+O(p^3)$). The \emph{spread} ratio
is $\frac{(\Delta-1)p(L+1)}{(\Delta p-1+(1-p)^\Delta)L+\Delta p}$. Writing
$A=(\Delta-1)p$, $B=\Delta p-1+(1-p)^\Delta$, $C=\Delta p$, this is
$A(L+1)/(BL+C)$, whose derivative with respect to $L$ has the sign of
$A(C-B)$; since $C-B=1-(1-p)^\Delta>0$, the ratio is strictly increasing in
$L$ for every finite $L\ge1$ and tends to its supremum $A/B=\frac{(\Delta-1)p}
{\Delta p-1+(1-p)^\Delta}$ only as $L\to\infty$ -- so at any finite $L$ the
spread ratio is strictly below this value, not at least it. Taking
$L\to\infty$ first (for fixed $\Delta,p$) and only then $p\to0$: the
denominator $B$ behaves like $\binom\Delta2p^2$ as $p\to0$ (the $O(p)$
terms cancel), so the $L\to\infty$ limit $A/B$ behaves like
$\frac{(\Delta-1)p}{\binom\Delta2p^2}=\frac{2}{\Delta p}\to\infty$ --
already unbounded at $\Delta=2$, independently of the saving ratio's
$\Delta\to\infty$ argument, but only once $L$ has been sent to infinity
first: for any \emph{fixed} $L$, the same ratio $A(L+1)/(BL+C)$ tends
instead to $A(L+1)/C=(\Delta-1)(L+1)/\Delta$ as $p\to0$ (since $B\to0$
faster than $C$), a finite constant, so the order of the two limits is not
interchangeable. The two ratios are genuinely different quantities -- the
savings' ratio bound does not apply to the spreads' ratio -- and both
statements above are established directly from the same exact closed
forms, with no approximation beyond the stated limits.\Halmos
\end{proof}

\begin{repeattheorem}[Theorem~\ref{thm:defer-complexity} (Complexity of DEFER)]
Let $n_t,m_t$ be the number of vertices and edges reachable from $F_t$ in
the union of the $\theta$ samples, $r$ the number of rounds, and $\alpha$
the inverse Ackermann function. With the dominator planner, round $t$
costs $O(\theta\,m_t\,\alpha(m_t,n_t))$ for the dominator trees plus
$O(b_t\,\theta'\,m_t\,\alpha)$ for the greedy picks; the total is
$O\bigl(\sum_t(1+b_t)\,\theta\,m_t\,\alpha\bigr)$, which is
$O(r\,b\,\theta\,m\,\alpha(m,n))$ in the worst case.
\end{repeattheorem}
\begin{proof}{Proof}
Each dominator tree is computed by the Cooper--Harvey--Kennedy iteration
on the reachable subgraph (near-linear; the Lengauer--Tarjan analysis gives
the $\alpha$ bound). Drawing $\theta$ live-edge samples reachable from
$F_t$ (Algorithm~\ref{alg:defer}) is a breadth-first search per sample. Recomputation after a pick is restricted to samples
whose cached reach set contains the picked vertex, which is exactly the
set of samples whose dominator tree can change. Summing over picks and
rounds gives the bound.\Halmos
\end{proof}

\begin{repeattheorem}[Theorem~\ref{thm:isocut-separation} (Isolation-move separation)]
There is a family of instances on which AdvancedGreedy, GreedyReplace, and
the Sandwich lower-bound greedy all save $O(b)$ vertices with budget $b$,
while a planner with frontier-isolation moves saves $\Omega(N)$ for
arbitrary $N$.
\end{repeattheorem}
\begin{proof}{Proof}
Construction: a decoy seed with $b+1$ out-neighbors, each with one private
leaf (single-vertex saving 2); a real seed with $c\le b$ out-neighbors, all
of which point to every vertex of a region $R$ of $N$ vertices whose
vertices have no private successors. Every single vertex in $R$ or among
the real seed's out-neighbors saves at most 1, so all three published
rules spend the budget on decoy neighbors (saving $2b$; GreedyReplace's
replacement pass replaces a decoy blocker by itself and stops), whereas
isolating the real seed saves $N+c$ at cost $c$.\Halmos
\end{proof}

\section{Proofs for Section~\ref{sec:twig} (TWIG)}\label{ec:twig-proofs}

\begin{repeatlemma}[Invariant maintained by \texttt{ExactSpread}]\label{ec:lem:invariant}
Fix $B$ and a step $i$. For every configuration $\kappa=(\Pi,\rho)$ with
$P_\kappa>0$ after step $i$,
\begin{align*}
P_\kappa=\Pr\nolimits_\omega\Bigl[\;&\Pi\text{ is the partition of }F_i\text{
by connectivity in }\bigl(F_i^{\ge},\,\omega|_{E_i}\bigr),\\
&\text{seed-containing part collapsed to SEED; and}\\
&\forall\text{ non-SEED block }b,\ \rho(b)=\bigl|
\{u:\mathrm{last}(u)\le i,\\
&\qquad u\text{ retired non-seed},\ u\sim_\omega b\}
\bigr|\;\Bigr],
\end{align*}
where $E_i$ is the set of edges processed by step $i$ (both endpoints
introduced, neither endpoint in $B$), $F_i^{\ge}=\{x\notin B:\mathrm{pos}(x)
\le i\}$ is every vertex introduced so far (active or already retired), and
$u\sim_\omega b$ means $u$ is connected, via live edges of $E_i$ within
$F_i^{\ge}$, to the current representative of block $b$. Moreover
$$\mathrm{acc}_i=\sum_{v\in V\setminus S\setminus B\,:\,\mathrm{last}(v)\le
i}\Pr\nolimits_\omega\bigl[v\sim_\omega\text{ some seed, using only edges of
}E_i\bigr].$$
\end{repeatlemma}
\begin{proof}{Proof}
By induction on $i$. \emph{Base case} $i=-1$ (before any step): $F_{-1}
=\varnothing$, the unique configuration is the empty partition with $P=1$,
$\mathrm{acc}=0$, and both sides of the claimed identities are vacuously
true ($E_{-1}=\varnothing$, no vertex has retired).

\emph{Introduce.} Adding $v$ as a new singleton block changes nothing about
already-active vertices' connectivity (no new edge is realized), and
correctly starts $v$'s own block (SEED if $v\in S$, pending $0$ otherwise,
since $v$ is trivially connected to itself and, being newly introduced, to
nothing else yet). The invariant is preserved for $\Pi$ and, since no new
edges enter $E_i$, for $\mathrm{acc}$.

\emph{Process\_edge$(u,v)$.} This is the only place a new edge $(u,v)$
enters $E_i$. Conditioning on its two outcomes (independent of every other
edge's realization, by the independent-cascade model's edge independence)
exactly reproduces the two branches: tails leaves $\Pi$, $\rho$,
$\mathrm{acc}$ untouched, matching that $(u,v)\notin\omega$ leaves
connectivity and every retired vertex's membership unchanged. Heads merges
$u$'s and $v$'s blocks, which is exactly the effect of adding a live edge to
a union-find-style connectivity structure. If the merge is into SEED: every
already-retired vertex $u'$ counted in the merging block's $\rho(b)$ is, by
the outer induction hypothesis, connected (via $E_i\setminus\{(u,v)\}$) to
$b$'s current representative, hence now (with $(u,v)$ live) transitively
connected to a seed for the first time in this history -- exactly one unit
each is owed to $\mathrm{acc}$, contributing $p(u,v)\cdot\rho(b)$ in
expectation over this branch, matching the update; $\rho(b)$ is then
correctly discharged (those vertices will never need to be paid for again,
since a vertex is reached at most once). If the merge is between two
ordinary blocks, no vertex's fate is decided yet (neither is connected to a
seed), so $\mathrm{acc}$ is untouched and the two blocks' pending vertex
sets simply become one, i.e.\ $\rho(b_1)+\rho(b_2)$, matching the update.

\emph{Retire$(u)$.} $u$ retiring means $\mathrm{last}(u)=i$: every edge
incident to $u$ is already in $E_i$, so $u$'s connectivity to everything
else active or retired is already fully determined and will never change.
If $u$'s block is already SEED: $u$ is reached with probability $P_\kappa$
in this history, contributed to $\mathrm{acc}$ unless $u$ itself is a seed
(seeds are excluded from ``reached,'' matching the definition of
$\FF(B)$). Otherwise $u$'s fate is not yet decided; deferring it into
$\rho(b)$ is exactly the claimed count (one more retired vertex now
connected to $b$), \emph{unless} $b$ has no other active member, in which
case $b$ can never merge with anything else in the future (its only
remaining edges were already all processed, by definition of retirement
having emptied it of active representatives) -- correctly discharging
vertices that are provably never reached. Removing $u$ from $\Pi$ matches
$F_i\setminus\{u\}$.\Halmos
\end{proof}

\begin{repeattheorem}[Theorem~\ref{thm:twig-correct} (Correctness of \texttt{ExactSpread})]
\texttt{ExactSpread}$(G,p,S)$.evaluate$(B)=\FF(B)$ for every $B$.
\end{repeattheorem}
\begin{proof}{Proof}
Apply Lemma~\ref{ec:lem:invariant} at $i=n-1$ (the last step): $F_{n-1}
=\varnothing$ (every vertex has retired by the time all its neighbors,
which include itself, have been introduced), $E_{n-1}=E\setminus(B$-incident
edges$)$, i.e.\ all of $G_B$'s edges, and every non-seed, non-blocked
vertex has $\mathrm{last}(v)\le n-1$. The second identity of
Lemma~\ref{ec:lem:invariant} then reads $\mathrm{acc}_{n-1}=
\sum_{v\in V\setminus S\setminus B}\Pr_\omega[v\sim_\omega\text{ some seed
in }G_B]=\FF(B)$, which is exactly what \texttt{evaluate}$(B)$
returns.\Halmos
\end{proof}

\begin{repeatproposition}[Proposition~\ref{prop:xp-space} (State space of \texttt{ExactSpread})]
A configuration's partition-plus-optional-SEED-designation component,
for a frontier of size $m\le w+1$, ranges over $B_{m+1}\le B_{w+2}$
possibilities, the $(w+2)$-th Bell number, independent of $n$: an
$m$-element set has $B_m$ (unmarked) partitions, and marking one block of a
given partition $\Pi$ as $\mathrm{SEED}$ -- or marking none -- gives
$\mathrm{blocks}(\Pi)+1$ distinct configurations from that one partition, so
summing over all partitions of the $m$-element frontier gives
$\sum_\Pi(\mathrm{blocks}(\Pi)+1)=B_{m+1}$ in total (the worked example of
Section~\ref{sec:twig-example} exhibits this directly: at $m=1$, the single
partition $\{\{2\}\}$ yields the two configurations ``$\{2\}$ ordinary'' and
``$\{2\}\equiv\mathrm{SEED}$,'' matching $B_2=2$). But each configuration's
pending vector has up to $w+1$ components, each ranging over
$\{0,\dots,n\}$, so the number of distinct configurations at any step is at
most $B_{w+2}\cdot(n+1)^{w+1}$.
\end{repeatproposition}

\begin{repeattheorem}[Theorem~\ref{thm:twig-collapse} (Correctness of the linear-aggregation collapse)]
Fix a partition shape $s$ and, at some step, let
$\mathcal K_s=\{(s,\rho):P_{(s,\rho)}>0\}$. Define the aggregates
$P_s=\sum_{(s,\rho)\in\mathcal K_s}P_{(s,\rho)}$ and
$W_b=\sum_{(s,\rho)\in\mathcal K_s}P_{(s,\rho)}\cdot\rho(b)$ for each
non-SEED block $b$ of $s$. Then $(P_s,\{W_b\})_s$ can be updated, at every
step, by the same four rules \texttt{ExactSpreadFast} implements, and this
update yields the identical sequence of \texttt{acc} increments as
\texttt{ExactSpread}, for every $B$.
\end{repeattheorem}
\begin{proof}{Proof}
We check each of the (at most) four ways a step transforms a configuration
$(s,\rho)\mapsto(s',\rho')$ within a fixed edge-outcome branch (shape
transitions never depend on $\rho$, only on $s$ and, for
\texttt{process\_edge}, the branch); linearity of each in $\rho$ is then
all that is needed.

\emph{Introduce a new non-seed block $b_0$.} $\rho'(b_0)=0$,
$\rho'(b)=\rho(b)$ otherwise, $s'=s\cup\{b_0\}$ is independent of $\rho$.
Then $W'_{b_0}=\sum_\rho P_\rho\cdot 0=0$ and $W'_b=W_b$ for $b\ne b_0$: no
update needed for existing entries, new entry is (implicitly) $0$.

\emph{Process\_edge, tails.} $s'=s$, $\rho'=\rho$, but the whole branch
carries probability weight $(1-p_e)$: $P'_s=(1-p_e)P_s$ and
$W'_b=(1-p_e)W_b$ for every entry.

\emph{Process\_edge, heads, merge into SEED.} The payout added to
$\mathrm{acc}$ in this branch is
$p_e\sum_{(s,\rho)}P_\rho\cdot\rho(b)=p_e\cdot W_b$ exactly (a direct
algebraic identity, not a mean-field substitution). Block $b$ is then
dropped ($\rho'$ has no entry for it).

\emph{Process\_edge, heads, merge two ordinary blocks $b_1,b_2\to b$.}
$\rho'(b)=\rho(b_1)+\rho(b_2)$, so
$W'_b=\sum_\rho P_\rho(\rho(b_1)+\rho(b_2))=W_{b_1}+W_{b_2}$.

\emph{Retire, block still active.} $\rho'(b)=\rho(b)+1$ for the retiring
block, others unchanged: $W'_b=\sum_\rho P_\rho(\rho(b)+1)=W_b+P_s$ (using
the aggregate $P_s$, not a per-history increment).

\emph{Retire, block emptied.} $b$'s entry is dropped for every history in
$\mathcal K_s$ simultaneously (the condition ``no other active member''
depends only on $s$, not $\rho$), so $W'_b$ is simply omitted.

\emph{Retire, block is SEED, $u\notin S$.} $\mathrm{acc}$ gains
$\sum_\rho P_\rho=P_s$ in this branch.

Each case is an exact algebraic identity between the aggregate before and
the aggregate after, with no term dropped or approximated; by induction
over the sequence of steps, $(P_s,\{W_b\})$ computed by
\texttt{ExactSpreadFast} therefore equals the shape-lumped aggregate of
\texttt{ExactSpread}'s full distribution at every step, and in particular
produces the identical sequence of contributions to $\mathrm{acc}$.\Halmos
\end{proof}

\begin{repeatproposition}[Proposition~\ref{prop:fpt-space} (State space of \texttt{ExactSpreadFast})]
The number of distinct states at any step is at most $B_{w+2}$ (one
$(P_s,\{W_b\})$ tuple per shape), independent of $n$.
\end{repeatproposition}

\begin{repeatproposition}[Proposition~\ref{prop:direction} (Star graphs separate forward and reversed elimination order)]
Let $T_k$ be the star $K_{1,k}$, $n=k+1$. Min-fill-in computes the
elimination order $\pi=(\ell_1,\dots,\ell_k,c)$, correctly certifying
treewidth $1$. Running the schedule of Section~\ref{sec:prelim} directly on
$\pi$ (the centre processed last) produces a frontier of size $k=n-1$
immediately before the centre's step. Running it on $\mathrm{reverse}(\pi)$
(the centre processed first) keeps the frontier at size at most $2$
throughout.
\end{repeatproposition}
\begin{proof}{Proof}
Immediate from the definitions of Section~\ref{sec:prelim}: under $\pi$,
$\mathrm{last}(\ell_i)=\mathrm{pos}(c)=k$ for every leaf (its only neighbor
$c$ is introduced last), so no leaf retires before step $k$, giving
$F_{k-1}=\{\ell_1,\dots,\ell_k\}$; under $\mathrm{reverse}(\pi)$,
$\mathrm{last}(\ell_i)=\mathrm{pos}(\ell_i)$ (once $c$ is already active,
$\ell_i$'s only edge is processed the instant $\ell_i$ is introduced, and
$c$ is not $\ell_i$'s own last neighbor to be introduced -- it already was),
so every leaf retires the step after it is introduced, and $c$ itself
retires only at the final step (its own $\mathrm{last}$ is the position of
the last-introduced leaf), giving $|F_i|\le 2$ for all $i$.\Halmos
\end{proof}

\section{Auxiliary Benchmark: Source Detection}\label{ec:source-experiments}

As a secondary, apples-to-apples benchmark motivated by the same
misinformation/epidemic-control setting as DEFER and TWIG (identifying

further), we also evaluate classical and learned source-detection methods
on identical, seed-fixed test instances, reproducing the methodology of
\citet{sterchi2026}. The thirteen methods: Random (na\"ive floor); Jordan

\subsection{Isolation moves in practice}\label{sec:isocut-practice}

\texttt{isocut} (AG extended with a frontier-isolation move) and
\texttt{defer\_cut} (DEFER wrapping \texttt{isocut}) are our own
constructions, not literature baselines. Theorem~\ref{thm:isocut-separation}
shows a worst-case family where isolation moves save $\Omega(N)$ against
every other rule's $O(b)$; Table~\ref{tab:isocut} checks whether this
shows up on the real SNAP-scale networks used throughout, and it does
not -- isocut is statistically indistinguishable from AG, and
defer\_cut is at best a marginal improvement (ca-HepTh) and a marginal
loss elsewhere. This reflects which graphs the adversarial construction's
decoy-seed structure resembles, not a failure of the proof: real
collaboration/infrastructure networks rarely have a seed whose entire
out-neighborhood points into an otherwise-unreachable region, so the move
is a documented worst-case possibility, not a default-algorithm

\subsection{TWIG optimality-gap study}
Random small graphs, $n=6$--$14$, edge density such that the realized
width $w$ (Section~\ref{sec:treewidth-prelim}) stays at most 5--6, budget
1--4, one to four seeds. \texttt{greedy\_exact} and
\texttt{brute\_force\_optimal\_block} both run with the identical exact
oracle \texttt{ExactSpreadFast.evaluate}, so any discrepancy is a true
optimality gap, not sampling noise. 60 instances were drawn; the oracle's
correctness was independently checked by exhaustive summation over all
$2^{|E|}$ live-edge subsets on graphs up to $n=9$ (2000+ test cases for
\texttt{ExactSpread}, 1000+ for \texttt{ExactSpreadFast}), matching
Theorems~\ref{thm:twig-correct} and \ref{thm:twig-collapse}.
